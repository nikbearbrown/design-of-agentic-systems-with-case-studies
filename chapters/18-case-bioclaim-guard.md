# Chapter 18 — Case: BioClaim Guard

*Verifying medical claims rather than generating them — PICO decomposition, live PubMed retrieval, and a four-state verdict that names UNCERTAIN as first-class output.*

**Author:** Darshak Desai

## Situation

A patient reads a confidently worded paragraph generated by a chatbot summarizing a study on aspirin and heart attacks. The paragraph is fluent. It cites no specific source. It is right in part — for adults at established cardiovascular risk, low-dose aspirin shows preventive benefit — and dangerously wrong as a universal statement, because for low-risk adults the [USPSTF 2022 recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/aspirin-to-prevent-cardiovascular-disease-preventive-medication) is *against* initiation due to bleeding risk. The fluent generic claim has erased the population qualifier. This is the silent failure BioClaim Guard was built to address. The system does not generate medical content — it verifies medical content already produced, by decomposing the claim into structured components, retrieving peer-reviewed studies, and emitting a four-state verdict tied to the retrieved evidence. The premise is that adding a verification layer to an existing AI-generated medical summary is cheaper than re-architecting the generator, and the verification layer's correctness properties are easier to specify than the generator's plausibility constraints.

## Architecture

The system is full-stack: React + Vite + Tailwind frontend, Flask 3.0 + Flask-CORS backend, four pipeline stages orchestrated by `ClaimVerifier`.

**Stage 1 — PICO Extractor.** Sends the claim to Claude with a structured prompt that decomposes it into Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome — the canonical [evidence-based-medicine framework](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/resources/ebm-tools/asking-focused-questions). Adds a `scope` field (universal, general, specific) and a `claim_type` field (prevention, treatment, diagnosis, harm, general). Universal-scope medical claims are flagged for downstream scrutiny because they are almost always overgeneralizations.

**Stage 2 — PubMed Service.** Builds an optimized search query from the PICO components — intervention and outcome terms only, with population included only when specific (*elderly patients*, *diabetic adults*) — and queries PubMed live via the [NCBI E-utilities API](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK25501/), retrieving the top five most relevant papers. Each paper's title, abstract, year, journal, authors, publication type, and DOI are parsed from the XML response. Study design is classified by priority: meta-analysis > systematic review > RCT > observational.

**Stage 3 — Claim Verifier.** Sends the PICO analysis and the retrieved abstracts back to Claude with a verification prompt that checks for seven specific failure modes — overgeneralization, population mismatch, causation-vs-correlation, study-design weakness (animal/in vitro evidence applied to human conclusions), effect-size exaggeration, outdated evidence, missing context. Returns a structured verdict.

**Stage 4 — Verdict.** One of four states: **Clinically Supported** (teal), **Misleading or Overgeneralized** (amber), **Contradicted** (red), **Insufficient Evidence** (slate). Rendered as a verdict card with a PICO breakdown grid, expandable evidence cards, study-type badges, and direct links to source PubMed records.

## Design rationale

The architectural commitment is **decomposition before evaluation**. A claim evaluated holistically — *is this medical statement true?* — is exactly the question a base LLM is bad at, because *true* is not a property the model has access to at generation time. The PICO decomposition turns one fuzzy question into four specific ones: which population, which intervention, compared to what, with what outcome? Each component is independently checkable against retrieved evidence. The seven-failure-mode checklist operationalizes the checking — overgeneralization is detectable by comparing the claim's `scope` against the populations in the retrieved abstracts; causation-vs-correlation is detectable by comparing the claim's verb against the study designs that support it.

The **live PubMed query** instead of a pre-built vector store is a deliberate trade. PubMed is updated daily; a static snapshot would produce false-confidence verdicts on claims about recent retractions or new evidence. The latency cost — 10 to 15 seconds per claim instead of milliseconds — is acceptable for a verification tool used at the moment of clinical decision-making, where the user has already stopped to ask the question. The PICO-driven query builder is the second-most-consequential design decision: early testing confirmed that submitting the full claim text as a PubMed query returned poor results because of conversational phrasing and overly specific language. Extracting only the most distinctive clinical terms — intervention and outcome — and dropping generic population descriptors that add noise restored retrieval precision.

The **four-state verdict** preserves the architecturally critical distinction Chapter 7 argued for: *no evidence found* is not the same as *evidence contradicts*. A binary classifier would collapse them. BioClaim Guard's *Insufficient Evidence* state is the explicit UNCERTAIN — and the colour code (slate, distinct from amber and red) makes it visually impossible to mistake for either form of failure.

## Trade-offs

The 10–15 second latency rules out inline use during real-time AI generation; the system is deployed as a verification step the user invokes deliberately. Live PubMed introduces hard dependencies on NCBI uptime and rate limits — the 340ms inter-request delay enforces the [E-utilities three-requests-per-second](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK25497/) ceiling without an API key, with empty-paper-list fallback to *Insufficient Evidence* on network error. The PICO query builder works well for treatment and prevention claims and less well for diagnostic claims where intervention is harder to extract; this is acknowledged in the documentation. Claude occasional output drift (markdown wrapping, preamble text) is handled by a fallback parser that strips fences and locates the first complete JSON object — a pragmatic but real maintenance surface.

## Outcomes and revisions

The current evaluation is an **8-claim proof-of-concept** covering all four classification categories: aspirin-for-all-adults (overgeneralization of RCT results), vitamin-C-cures-colds (direct contradiction by Cochrane review), omega-3-prevents-Alzheimer's (insufficient evidence for prevention), exercise-prevents-diabetes-in-overweight-adults (well-supported intervention). The author reports *evaluation design and targets, not post-hoc fabricated numbers* — a commitment that puts the project in the right column of the chapter's distinction between honest reporting and confident silence. Targets are: classification accuracy ≥ 75%, retrieval recall ≥ 70%, faithfulness ≥ 80%, clinical agreement ≥ 75%. The 50-claim expanded evaluation with two-reviewer labeling and inter-rater agreement is the next planned step, along with a **baseline comparison** running plain Claude on the same 50 claims without PICO decomposition and without PubMed retrieval, to quantify exactly how much the pipeline improves over a single-prompt baseline. This is the right second study; it is what separates *the architecture works* from *the architecture works better than careful prompting alone could buy.*

## Pattern connection

This is Chapter 7's Fact Check List Pattern in clinical-evidence form. PICO is the structured-output schema; the seven-failure-mode checklist is the verification rubric; the four-state verdict — Supported / Misleading-or-Overgeneralized / Contradicted / Insufficient Evidence — is exactly the PASS / FAIL / UNCERTAIN distinction the chapter argued is non-negotiable.

## Transfer prompt

In your own claim-verification system, what is the structured decomposition of a claim that lets you check it component-by-component rather than holistically? What does the *Insufficient Evidence* state look like in your output, and is it visually distinct from both PASS and FAIL? When the underlying knowledge source is updated daily, are you querying it live — or paying the false-confidence cost of a stale vector store?

## A note about AI

BioClaim Guard is an agent that evaluates biomedical claims. The claim space is enormous and the model has read summary statements rather than primary studies.

Where the model genuinely helps: producing the canonical methodological objections to a claim of a given type.

Where the model does damage: declaring a biomedical claim true or false. The verdict depends on primary literature the model has summarized rather than read.

The rule: methodological vocabulary from the model; truth verdicts from primary sources, named and linked.
